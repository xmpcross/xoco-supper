Ground spice is a countdown. The moment a seed is cracked open its aromatic oils start leaving, and a jar of ground cumin that smelled of something in the shop smells of dust within three months. Whole seeds keep for a year and often two, and grinding them takes less time than finding the jar did.

So this kitchen buys six things whole and grinds them as it goes. Everything else — paprika, turmeric, the chilli flakes — is bought ground because there is no sensible way to do otherwise, and bought small so it gets used before it fades.

## The six

**Cumin.** The one that goes furthest. Toasted and cracked over [a pot of chickpeas](/post/a-pot-of-chickpeas-worth-the-soak/), rubbed on anything that is about to be roasted, stirred into yogurt.

**Coriander seed.** Sweeter and more citrus than the leaf, and the reason [the dukkah on the carrots](/post/roast-carrots-with-yogurt-and-dukkah/) tastes of anything. Buy it whole or do not bother.

**Fennel seed.** For pork, for sausage meat, for a tray of roasting vegetables that has gone quiet. A small pinch in bread dough is a trick worth knowing.

**Black pepper.** The one everyone already grinds fresh, and the proof that the principle works: nobody who has tasted the difference goes back to the tin.

**Cardamom.** Green pods, cracked open, seeds ground. Rice, milky puddings, and the coffee. Pre-ground cardamom is the fastest-fading spice there is.

**Chilli, dried whole.** Not a seed, but the same logic. A dried chilli toasted for thirty seconds and crumbled has a depth the flakes in the jar never had.

## The two-minute habit

Heat a small dry pan over medium heat. Add the seeds, shake the pan for a minute or so until they smell toasted and one or two start to pop, then tip them into a mortar or a cheap electric grinder. Grind, and use straight away.

That is the whole technique. It is the difference between a spice you can smell from across the room and one you have to lean into the jar to find, and it costs nothing but the pan being hot.

## Storing them

Away from the stove. A rack over the hob looks right and is the worst place in the house for a spice, because heat is what the oils leave in response to. A drawer or a cupboard door, whole seeds in the jars they came in, and the date written on the lid so a two-year-old jar of coriander can be spotted and replaced.
