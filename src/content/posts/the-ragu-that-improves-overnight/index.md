Almost every long-cooked meat sauce tastes better the next day, and the usual explanation — that the flavours "meld" — is doing a lot of work without saying anything. What actually happens is more interesting and it changes how you cook it.

Overnight in the fridge, the fat that separated during the long simmer solidifies on the surface. When you reheat it gently and stir, that fat re-disperses into the liquid as much finer droplets than it managed the first time, and finely dispersed fat reads on the palate as body and roundness. It is an emulsion that has had twelve hours to sort itself out.

## Three additions, in this order

The order is not tradition for its own sake. Tomato paste goes in early and gets fried, which drives off its raw metallic edge and deepens it. Milk goes in next, while the meat is still relatively firm, so the lactose has hours to brown and the proteins have hours to work on the mince. Wine follows to deglaze and lift. Tomatoes come last, because acid tightens protein and putting them in early gives you meat that never fully softens.

Reverse any two of those and you will still eat well, but you will taste the difference.

## Why the soffritto takes twenty minutes

Onion, carrot, and celery cooked for five minutes taste of onion, carrot, and celery. Cooked for twenty over a low heat, with salt drawing the water out, they collapse into something sweet and unidentifiable that reads as depth rather than as vegetables. This is the free flavour in the recipe and it costs nothing but patience.

## Browning in batches

A kilo of mince dropped into a pan at once drops the temperature below 100C and the meat simply poaches in its own juice. Grey mince cannot be fixed later. Two or three batches, each left undisturbed for a minute before breaking up, gets you the crust that the entire sauce is built on.

## The pasta is not an afterthought

Tagliatelle or rigatoni, cooked two minutes short, finished in the sauce with a ladle of the water. A ragù served _on_ pasta rather than tossed _through_ it is a different and worse dish, and it takes the same amount of time.
