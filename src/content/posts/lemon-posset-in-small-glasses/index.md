A posset is the oldest kind of trick: three ingredients, one pan, and a dessert that sets without eggs, gelatine, or agar. It has been made in England since at least the fifteenth century, and the mechanism was understood a long time after the recipe was.

## What is actually happening

Cream is an emulsion of fat droplets in water, stabilised by casein proteins. Casein is stable at neutral pH; drop the pH below about 4.6 — which lemon juice does immediately — and the proteins lose their charge, stop repelling each other, and link into a network that traps the liquid. It is the same reaction that makes yogurt and fresh cheese, arrested at the point where it is a gel rather than a curd.

The boiling is what makes it firm rather than merely thick. Three minutes at a rolling boil drives off enough water to raise the fat and solids concentration to the point where that protein network has something to hold.

## Three failures, all avoidable

Not enough fat. Below about 48% there is simply not enough to build structure, and you get lemon cream soup. Read the carton.

Not enough boiling. The most common one. Three minutes at a genuine rolling boil, not a simmer, and time it.

Juice added too hot. Straight into a boiling pan, the proteins denature and clump rather than forming an even network, and the texture goes grainy. A minute off the heat is enough.

## Small glasses

Six hundred millilitres of double cream divided six ways is 100ml each, and that is the correct portion. It is one of the richest things in this section and a large bowl of it defeats people halfway down.

Something thin and crisp beside it — shortbread, a tuile, a plain biscuit. The contrast is most of the pleasure.
