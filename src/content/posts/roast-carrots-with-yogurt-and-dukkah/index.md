This is a plate built on one idea, which is that hot food on cold food is more interesting than either on its own. The carrots come out of a 220C oven and go straight onto yogurt that has been in the fridge, and for the first two minutes on the table there is a genuine temperature gradient in every mouthful.

It is a trick borrowed from a hundred Levantine and Turkish dishes, and it works with almost any roasted vegetable. Carrots are the version I make most because they are always in the house and because they concentrate so obligingly.

## Sweetness by subtraction

A carrot is around nine per cent sugar and a great deal of water. Roasted hard, the water leaves and the sugar stays, so what tasted mild raw tastes almost candied after half an hour. Push it further than feels safe: proper black at the tips is where the flavour is, and pale roast carrots are the most common way this goes wrong.

Halving thick carrots lengthways is not about cooking time. It is about creating a flat face that can sit against hot metal.

## Honey at the end

Sugar added at the start of a 220C roast is burnt sugar by minute fifteen, and burnt sugar is bitter rather than sweet. A teaspoon tossed through in the last ten minutes glazes rather than scorches.

## The garlic has to be a paste

Grated on a microplane and worked with salt until it is genuinely a paste, not chopped. Raw garlic in visible pieces in cold yogurt is harsh and keeps getting harsher; dispersed as a paste it is savoury and even.

## Dukkah, and why it should stay coarse

The point of dukkah is that it crunches. Blitzing it in a processor releases oil from the hazelnuts and you end up with something closer to a wet crumb. Chop by hand, keep it uneven, and add it at the last second so it stays dry on top of the yogurt.
