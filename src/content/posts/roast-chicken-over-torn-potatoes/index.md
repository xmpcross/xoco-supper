A roast chicken has one difficult requirement — skin that shatters over meat that is still juicy — and two things that are usually blamed for missing it: the oven and the bird. Neither is normally the problem. The problem is water.

Chicken skin cannot crisp while it is losing moisture, because evaporation holds the surface at around 100C and browning needs considerably more than that. So the useful work happens the night before, in the fridge, with salt.

## Dry brining, in one paragraph

Salt on the surface draws water out of the skin by osmosis, then dissolves into it and is drawn back into the flesh, carrying seasoning with it. Overnight, uncovered, the fridge's dry air takes the surface moisture away for good. What you have in the morning is a bird seasoned to the bone with skin like parchment, and it will brown in half the time a wet one would.

One per cent of the bird's weight is the number. For a 1.8kg chicken that is 18g, or about two level teaspoons of fine salt.

## Tearing the potatoes

Parboil, drain, steam dry, then tear. A torn potato has a rough, fractured surface with far more area than a cut one, and every one of those ragged edges is somewhere fat can collect and fry. Under a chicken, that fat is rendered chicken fat, and the result is closer to a roast potato than anything a separate tray achieves.

Floury varieties — Maris Piper, russet, King Edward — tear well. Waxy ones resist and stay smooth.

## The two temperatures

Twenty minutes at 220C sets and starts the skin while the inside is still cold. Dropping to 190C for the rest means the breast comes up to temperature gently instead of tightening. It is a small thing and it is the difference between good and slightly dry.

## No gravy

There is a tin with rendered fat, chicken juices, collapsed garlic, and a roasted lemon in it. Squeezing the lemon and scraping the tin gives you something better than a flour-thickened gravy would, in ninety seconds, and it tastes of the bird rather than of stock.
