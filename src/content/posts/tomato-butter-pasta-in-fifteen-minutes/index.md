This is a sauce with four ingredients and one instruction, and it is better than most of what I make with twenty. The debt is obvious and worth stating: Marcella Hazan published the tomato, butter, and onion version decades ago and nobody has improved on it since.

What I have changed is only the ratio and the finish — more butter than she used, and the pasta tossed in the pan rather than sauced in a bowl.

## Why the onion goes in the bin

Halved and left cut-side down in the simmering sauce, an onion gives up its sugars and its aromatics without ever contributing texture. By the end it is soft, spent, and slightly grey. Eating it adds nothing; leaving it in the pan makes a sauce that tastes sweeter and rounder than anything you can do with soffritto in the same twenty minutes.

If throwing it away offends you, chop it into a frittata the next morning.

## The butter is structural

A hundred grams sounds like a lot because it is a lot. But butter is roughly eighty per cent fat and sixteen per cent water, and when it meets starchy pasta water in a hot pan it emulsifies into something that coats every tube. Cut it back to fifty and you get a thin, acidic tomato sauce that slides off the pasta and pools at the bottom of the bowl.

## Two minutes short

Pasta pulled early finishes in the sauce, and while it does it releases starch into the pan and absorbs tomato instead of water. That last minute of hard tossing over heat is the only technique in the recipe, and it is the one that makes it look like restaurant food.

## What else it is for

Double the sauce and freeze half. It is the base for a tray of baked pasta, the sauce for a quick shakshuka, and the thing to have on a night when [the shopping list](/post/six-dinners-one-shopping-list/) has run out.
