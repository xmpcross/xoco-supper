Olive oil is the ingredient this site uses most and the one that is easiest to get wrong, because the labelling is a mixture of legal definitions, meaningless flourishes, and outright noise.

You do not need to become an expert. You need to know which four things on the bottle are load-bearing.

## 1. A harvest date, not a best-before

This is the single most useful thing on a label, and most bottles do not have one. Olive oil is a fruit juice and it degrades from the day it is pressed — its polyphenols and aromatics fall off steadily, and by eighteen months a good oil tastes like a mediocre one.

A best-before date tells you when the producer estimates it will be unpleasant. A harvest date tells you how old it actually is. Look for one within the last year, and treat its absence as a mild warning sign.

## 2. Dark glass or a tin

Light degrades olive oil faster than anything except heat. Clear glass on a lit supermarket shelf is a bottle that has been slowly ruined in front of you. Dark green glass or a metal tin means somebody in the supply chain was paying attention.

Which also means: decant nothing into a clear cruet by the hob. That is the worst place in the kitchen for it.

## 3. One country of origin, ideally one region

"Packed in Italy" is not an origin. Neither is "a blend of EU and non-EU olive oils", which appears on a great deal of what is sold as Italian and means the contents could be from four countries. It is not necessarily bad oil, but nobody is standing behind it.

A single country is better. A named region, estate, or cultivar is better still, because at that point somebody's name is on it.

## 4. "Extra virgin" and nothing else

Extra virgin is a legal grade with defined chemistry — free acidity below 0.8%, no refining, no heat, no solvents. It is the only grade worth buying for anything, including cooking.

Everything after those words is decoration. "Cold pressed" is redundant: extra virgin is by definition mechanically extracted at low temperature. "First press" is meaningless with modern centrifuges — there is only one press. "Pure", "light", and "extra light" all denote _refined_ oil, which is a lower grade despite sounding like an upgrade.

## What the taste should do

Open a good bottle and it should smell green — cut grass, tomato leaf, artichoke, sometimes banana or almond. Taste a spoonful and there should be bitterness in the middle of your tongue and a peppery catch at the back of your throat, sometimes enough to make you cough.

That pepper is oleocanthal, and it is a direct signal of polyphenol content. It fades with age. An oil with no bitterness and no catch is either old, refined, or from olives picked very ripe — and it will contribute nothing to a dish beyond fat.

## Two bottles, not one

The practical conclusion is to stop looking for one oil that does everything. Buy a large tin of decent, inexpensive extra virgin for the pan, where heat will drive off the delicate aromatics regardless of what you paid. And buy one good bottle, with a harvest date on it, for finishing — over [beans](/post/white-bean-stew-with-lemon-and-dill/), over bread, over a tomato salad, where you will actually taste it.

The good bottle should feel slightly extravagant and it should be finished within three months. A bottle of expensive oil kept for special occasions is a bottle of expensive oil going stale.
