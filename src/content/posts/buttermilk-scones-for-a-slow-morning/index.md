A scone is an argument about butter. Everything else on the list is a rounding error.

The goal is discrete pieces of solid butter still present in the dough when it enters the oven. In the heat, each one melts and its water content flashes to steam, pushing the surrounding dough apart and leaving a void. Hundreds of those voids read as flakiness and lift. Butter that has been warmed and rubbed into the flour, by contrast, coats the starch and gives you something short and sandy — pleasant, but a shortbread, not a scone.

## Why grating works

Rubbing in by hand takes minutes, during which your hands are transferring heat. A frozen block on a box grater produces uniform shards in about twenty seconds, and the butter never softens. Toss them through with a fork and they stay separate.

## Undermixed is correct

You are looking for a dough that still has dry flour in it when you stop stirring. Every extra turn of the knife develops gluten and, worse, smears the butter. The folding step then brings it together and builds layers at the same time — three letter folds is plenty, and each one should feel slightly less shaggy than the last.

If the dough looks smooth and elastic, it is already too late.

## Cut, do not twist

A twisted cutter drags the edge of the dough round and pinches the layers closed. Straight down and straight up, with a floured cutter, leaves the sides open so the layers can climb.

## Heat

220C, and get the oven there properly. Scones need the butter to melt and the steam to be generated faster than the structure sets; a moderate oven gives the butter time to leak out into the tray instead, and you get flat, greasy discs.
