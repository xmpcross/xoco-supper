Kitchen shops sell solutions to problems most people do not have. The genuine list of things you need to cook everything on this site is three items long, and two of them are worth spending real money on.

## The knife

One chef's knife, 20cm, and it should be the most expensive thing in your kitchen after the pan. Not a set — a block of six knives at the price of one good one is six knives you will not enjoy using.

What matters is that it is comfortable in your hand and that you keep it sharp. A cheap knife kept sharp outperforms an expensive one that is blunt, every time, which means the honing rod and the occasional whetstone or sharpening service are part of the purchase rather than accessories to it.

A blunt knife is also the more dangerous one. It requires force, force slips, and it crushes rather than cuts — which is why bruised, blackening herbs are usually a sharpening problem rather than a technique problem.

## The board

Wood or a thick composite, and as large as your counter will take. The most common mistake is a board too small to work on, which turns every prep job into a series of clearing operations.

Not glass. Not stone. Both destroy an edge in a handful of uses, and both are why some people believe good knives do not stay sharp.

## The pan

One heavy, oven-safe frying pan, 28 to 30cm, in cast iron or thick carbon steel. Weight is the point. [Charred broccoli](/post/charred-broccoli-with-anchovy-butter/), [seared cabbage](/post/charred-cabbage-with-brown-butter/), and [blistered peppers](/post/blistered-shishito-peppers-with-lime/) all depend on the pan holding its temperature when cold food lands in it, and a thin pan simply cannot. It drops to a temperature at which food steams and stays there.

Oven-safe matters because half of the useful things you can do involve starting on the hob and finishing in the oven.

If you buy a second pan, make it a non-stick 24cm for eggs, and accept that it is a consumable that will need replacing every few years.

## Then, in order

A digital scale. Baking without one is guesswork, and [the sourdough](/post/everyday-sourdough-without-the-ceremony/) and the cakes are written in grams for a reason. Twelve pounds, and it makes you a better baker the day it arrives.

A heavy casserole with a lid, for [braises](/post/short-ribs-braised-in-red-wine/), beans, and bread. Enamelled cast iron if the budget allows; a plain lidded cast-iron pot if not.

A rimmed sheet pan, or two. Rimmed, so the fat stays on it.

A box grater and a microplane. Between them: butter for scones, garlic to a paste, citrus zest, parmesan.

A mortar and pestle, for whole spices. Small, heavy, unglazed inside.

## Things I have given away

A garlic press, which produces harsh, uneven garlic and eleven small holes to clean. An avocado tool. A herb stripper. An egg slicer. A mango splitter. A banana slicer, received as a gift and given onwards without ceremony.

An electric spiraliser. A milk frother. A stand mixer, which I used for two years and replaced with a bowl and a spoon for everything except meringue, and I do not make meringue.

A set of nesting measuring cups, once I started weighing things.

## The pattern

Almost every gadget replaces a knife with something that does one job worse. The exceptions are the ones that do something a knife genuinely cannot — a microplane, a mandoline, a scale — and those are the ones still in the drawer.
