Raw fennel divides people, and almost every conversion I have managed came down to thickness. Cut into wedges it is aggressive, fibrous, and unmistakably of aniseed. Shaved to a millimetre it is delicate, faintly sweet, and mostly a texture.

The rest of the dish is a lesson in not wasting anything: the juice that runs out while you segment the oranges is the dressing, and it is better than anything you would have built from a bottle.

## The mandoline argument

A mandoline gets you consistent, translucent slices in ninety seconds and is worth owning for this alone. A sharp knife will do it, but "as thin as you can" with a knife usually means two or three millimetres, and that is a different salad.

If you are using a knife, halve the bulb through the root first so you have a flat face to stabilise it, and slice across the grain.

## Iced water

Fennel is mostly water in rigid cells. Sliced thin, those cells lose turgor within minutes and the slices go limp. Ten minutes in iced water reverses it — the cells take water back up by osmosis and the slices curl and stiffen.

Then they have to be dried, thoroughly, in a towel or a spinner. Wet fennel dilutes the dressing and it will pool in the bottom of the bowl.

## Honey against the fennel

A teaspoon is not there to sweeten the salad. It is there because fennel's anise note reads as slightly bitter, and a small amount of sugar rounds that off without making the dressing taste sweet. Leave it out and the whole thing is sharper and less friendly.

## Where it goes

Beside anything rich. It is the correct thing next to [the roast chicken](/post/roast-chicken-over-torn-potatoes/), and it cuts through the short ribs better than any green salad would.
