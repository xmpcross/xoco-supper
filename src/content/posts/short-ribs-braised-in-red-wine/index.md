Short ribs are what you buy when you want the effect of a rib of beef for a quarter of the money. They are almost entirely collagen and intramuscular fat, which makes them useless for anything fast and close to unfailable over four hours.

The whole recipe is two decisions: brown them harder than feels sensible, and do not drown them.

## Browning is not a step to hurry

Eight to ten minutes a batch, in a pan hot enough to be slightly alarming. Every bit of dark crust is Maillard product, and Maillard product is where the sauce's colour and about half its flavour live. A pale sear gives you a pale, one-note braise that no amount of stock will rescue.

The light dusting of flour is there for two reasons: it dries the surface further, so browning starts sooner, and it thickens the finished liquid enough that you will not need to do anything about it later.

## Why the wine gets reduced

A bottle of wine is roughly thirteen per cent alcohol and a lot of acid. Both need driving off before the long cook, because alcohol left in a covered pot cannot evaporate and stays as a raw, solvent note through the whole braise. Boiled down by half first, the wine turns into concentrated fruit and tannin.

Use something you would drink a glass of. Not because it needs to be expensive, but because anything with an off note will have four hours to concentrate.

## Two-thirds, not covered

This is the difference between a braise and a stew. With the top of the meat above the liquid, that surface continues to brown and dry in the oven's heat while the underside gently poaches, and the two textures are what makes a braise taste layered. Fully submerged, everything comes out uniformly soft.

## The day-ahead trick

Cool it, refrigerate it, and lift the solid fat cap off the next morning. You will remove more fat in thirty seconds than twenty minutes of skimming a hot pot achieves, and the sauce will be glossier for it. Then reheat gently, reduce, and serve.
