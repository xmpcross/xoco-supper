Focaccia is the most forgiving bread there is, and it is still the one people most often get wrong — because the two things that make it work both feel like mistakes while you are doing them.

The first is the water. At eighty per cent hydration this dough does not form a ball and cannot be kneaded. It is stirred, folded a few times, and then left in the fridge overnight, where time does the work that hands would otherwise have to.

The second is the oil. Four tablespoons in the tray is not a mistake and it is not a typo. It is a shallow fry, and it is the difference between a crisp, lacquered base and a flatbread.

## What the overnight rise buys you

Cold fermentation slows the yeast down and lets enzymes get on with breaking starch into sugar. That sugar is what browns, and it is where the faintly sweet, almost nutty note in good focaccia comes from. A dough proved warm in three hours rises just as well and tastes of very little.

Eighteen hours is the outside edge. Past that the gluten starts giving up and the crumb goes gummy.

## Dimpling, and why it has to be firm

Press with the pads of your fingers, straight down, until you feel the tray. Timid dimples close up in the oven and you get a dome. Real dimples hold oil, and the oil in them is what fries those pockets darker than the rest of the top.

Anything you push into those wells should be able to take twenty-five minutes at 230C. Halved tomatoes can. Sliced tomatoes cannot, and neither can garlic.

## Eating it

Warm, torn, on its own. Or under [the tomato salad](/post/the-tomato-salad-that-needs-nothing-else/), where it does the job bread was invented for.
