Stone fruit sold in the north is picked hard so it survives the journey, and a good proportion of it never gets sweet — it goes from hard to mealy without passing through ripe. That fruit is not a loss, it is just a different recipe.

## Heat as a substitute for sun

Ripening on the tree converts starch to sugar and breaks pectin down, which is why a ripe peach is both sweet and soft. Twenty-five minutes at 200C does an approximation of both: water evaporates so the sugar that is there concentrates, and pectin degrades so the flesh collapses.

It will not turn a bad plum into a good one. It will turn a bad plum into a good pudding, which is a better outcome than the fruit bowl was going to manage.

## A snug dish

This matters more than it sounds. Fruit spread out in a large tray gives up its juice into a thin layer that evaporates and then burns, and you get dry fruit and a black dish. Packed tightly, the same juice pools deep enough to stay liquid and reduces into a syrup.

## Water in the base

Two tablespoons, and this is the one measurement in the recipe that does not scale. Honey on its own in a hot oven will be caramelising before the fruit has released anything, and caramelised honey is bitter. The water buys the fruit five minutes to start giving up liquid of its own.

## Thyme, not cinnamon

Cinnamon, star anise, and vanilla all work and all make it taste like a dessert. Thyme keeps it ambiguous — it is just as good spooned over yogurt at breakfast, or alongside cheese, or next to something roasted. Savoury herbs with sweet fruit is one of the most reliable moves there is.
