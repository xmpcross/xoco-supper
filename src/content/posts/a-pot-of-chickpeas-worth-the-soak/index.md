There is nothing wrong with a tin of chickpeas — [the bean stew](/post/white-bean-stew-with-lemon-and-dill/) is built on tins and it is a good dish. But a chickpea cooked from dried in an aromatic, oily broth is not the same ingredient. It is creamier, it holds its shape while being soft all the way through, and it tastes of something.

The reason to make a whole pot is that the effort does not scale. Five hundred grams takes exactly as much of your attention as one tin, and it feeds you for a week.

## Salt in the soaking water

For years the received wisdom was that salt toughens the skins. It is the opposite. Sodium ions displace the calcium and magnesium that hold pectin in the cell walls rigid, so salted-soaked pulses cook faster and more evenly, with skins that soften at the same rate as the interior rather than bursting first.

A tablespoon in two litres is enough. Rinse before cooking, since the soaking water carries away some of the compounds that make legumes hard work.

## The ten-minute hard boil

A short violent boil at the start pushes foam and scum to the surface, where you can skim it, and gets the pot to temperature quickly. After that, hard boiling only breaks the chickpeas up. Everything else happens at a simmer so lazy you have to look closely to see it.

## Cooked means cooked

A chickpea that is soft on the outside and faintly chalky in the middle will ruin whatever it goes into and cannot be corrected by more cooking once it has cooled. Test three from different depths, and when in doubt give it another fifteen minutes. Over-cooked chickpeas make excellent hummus; under-cooked ones make nothing.

## What to do with them

Warm, in a bowl of their own broth with olive oil and pepper, is dinner. Drained and roasted hard, they are a snack. Blitzed with tahini and lemon they are the best hummus you will make, and they go straight under [the whipped feta](/post/whipped-feta-with-hot-honey/) on a table before dinner.
